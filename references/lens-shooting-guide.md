# Lens-Category Shooting Guide (Sony E/FE mount)

Reference by category. Effective focal length = stated length × the body's crop factor (1.5x on APS-C, 1.0x on full-frame). The effective ranges below assume APS-C; on a full-frame body the effective range equals the stated focal length. Check `references/camera-bodies.md` for the body's stabilization, burst rate and card setup before quoting handheld or burst numbers.

## Fast primes (e.g. 35mm f/1.8, 50mm f/1.8, 85mm f/1.8)

- On APS-C the effective range is typically 50–130mm, which is portrait and general-purpose territory.
- Shoot 1–2 stops down from wide open (e.g. f/2.5–f/4 on an f/1.8 lens) for reliable sharpness on the subject; wide open is fine for eyes-in-focus portraits where background blur is the point.
- Use Eye AF with single-point or Zone AF for portraits; Wide AF tends to grab the wrong plane on a shallow-DOF prime.
- Handheld floor: aim for a shutter speed of at least 1/(stated focal length × crop factor) as a starting point, e.g. about 1/80s for a 50mm on APS-C. Stabilization (IBIS and OSS where present) lets you go slower; most primes have no OSS, so the body's IBIS does the work, and a body without IBIS has nothing to fall back on.

## Standard zooms (e.g. 16-55mm f/2.8, 18-135mm)

- On APS-C the effective range is roughly 24–200mm, the "one lens for the day" option.
- For general use, f/4–f/5.6 balances sharpness and light; open up to the lens's maximum aperture in low light before pushing ISO into the range where the body's sensor gets noisy.
- Wide AF or Zone AF works well since subjects are often more dynamic.

## Telephoto / wildlife (e.g. 70-350mm, 100-400mm)

- On APS-C the effective range is roughly 105–600mm and beyond.
- Shutter speed matters more than aperture for freezing motion. For birds and wildlife, 1/1000s or faster is a safer floor than the handheld-focal-length rule.
- Use continuous AF (AF-C) with Zone or Tracking AF and the body's fastest drive mode for action. Subject-tracking quality varies by body generation.
- Electronic shutter can help hit higher frame rates on some bodies but watch for rolling-shutter distortion on fast-moving subjects such as wings and vehicles.

## Macro (e.g. 30mm macro, 90mm macro)

- Effective range depends on the body and lens; working distance matters more than focal length for skittish subjects (insects), so longer macro lenses give more room.
- Stop down more than usual (f/8–f/11) since depth of field at macro distances is extremely shallow even at moderate apertures.
- AF can hunt at close range. Manual focus with focus peaking is often more reliable than AF-C for true macro work.
- A tripod or focus stacking helps more here than stabilization does.

## Wide-angle / astro (e.g. 10-18mm, 11mm f/1.8)

- On APS-C the effective range is roughly 15–27mm.
- For astro and Milky Way work: the widest aperture available, ISO 1600–3200 as a starting point, and a shutter speed from the "500 rule" adjusted for crop: 500 / (stated focal length × crop factor) ≈ the longest exposure before stars trail (an 11mm lens on APS-C is effectively about 16.5mm, so roughly 30s). Higher-resolution sensors show trailing sooner, so shorten the exposure if stars look stretched at 100%.
- Use manual focus with magnified live view on a bright star to nail infinity focus. AF is unreliable in the dark.
- Turn IBIS off for long exposures on a tripod; with no shake to correct it can introduce blur.

## Video-specific notes (any lens)

- Watch focus breathing on cheaper primes and zooms. Some bodies offer breathing compensation and some don't; without it a lens that breathes will visibly shift framing when pulling focus. Check the body's spec before promising it.
- OSS lenses generally give smoother handheld video than IBIS alone on bodies that have both; combine them (Active SteadyShot or the body's equivalent) for the steadiest handheld footage.
- At 4K, keep an eye on rolling shutter with fast pans, especially on telephoto lenses, and check whether the body's higher frame-rate 4K modes crop the sensor.
